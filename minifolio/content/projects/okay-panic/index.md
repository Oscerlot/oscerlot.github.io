# This game is bad!
- It's currently sitting at 'Mostly Negative' on Steam, and it deserves to be there... So it's kind of weird that I'm putting it here, right?
- Well, the story behind the development is interesting, and it personally taught me so much that I enjoy sharing the failure that is this game.
- The time between the initial idea, and being purchasable to Steam was **only 6 weeks.** But that wasn't really by choice.


<div style="background-image: url('/microfolio/content/projects/okay-panic/images/Screenshot_1.png'); height: 300px; background-size: cover;"></div>

## So.. why make it?
- Due to some previous work, I would get sent a bunch of VR hardware, which usually wasn't anything novel. But guess what I received in the mail? A couple pairs of the Valve Index Controllers dev kits... only 6 weeks before they were officially released.
- Now, if you weren't around at the time, these things were crazy cool. Worked with your existing HTC Vive hardware, finger and pressure tracking, you could actually feel like you were throwing things. Crazy times. 
- I felt these were going to be so popular, and had to be first to market with a novel game that utilized them, and since I had a couple pairs I roped in a friend to help.
- We ended up, naturally, landing on a microgame-game due to the new features and came up with a bunch of interactions, like popping balloons by squeezing them, and getting the visceral tactile feel of it popping. A lot of these things worked very, very well. 
- But again, this was 6 weeks from start to end, including marketing, and release management, and we both also had full time jobs working in games.
- To speed some things up, I got a contractor to do the 2D and graphics, we rounded out the art with a bunch of existing assets we had, and others we found shared under a CC licence online.
- What drove a lot of the development was also about what looked marketable, and what could we make 5-10 clips out of fun and weird moments and share it on Twitter and Reddit to get some interest, which is exactly what we did.

## Wait, people are noticing...
- So, I was kind of right, people did like what you can do with these controllers. It was unlike watching previous VR experiences. You could telegraph actual emotion though the hand movements, which resonated with a lot of people. 
- The clips tended to perform well on VR game subreddits, getting a somewhat decent 50-100 wishlists per post.
- Mind you, this is all while the game was in shambles. We had a bunch of microgames and interactions, but we still didn't have a good cohesive experience of tying this all together.
- Then, one clip in particular I made and posted to r/gaming hit the front page of [Reddit](https://www.reddit.com/r/gaming/comments/c5q2q5/theres_nothing_more_unsettling_than_poking_out/) and amassed well over 1,000,000 views. 
- Lad Bible featured it at the time, and I even had people in my life, completely unrelated to games, mentioned seeing it without knowing it was ours. 
- You think that this would then convert to crazy wishlists, right? Given that the prior, smaller posts were getting a decent amount?
- It was... **about 10**. Yes, 10 wishlists.

- Granted, there wasn't a watermark or any call to action due to the rules of the subreddit, so people did have to try and seek out what it was - where you could find a link to the Steam page on the second comment in the post. 

- But this all makes sense in hindsight really though. A niche game on a niche platform, that require an extra set of niche controllers, and requires some interest in then looking for what it is to go and wishlist it.
- This was a great learning moment of how targeting the right audience, even being tiny, is still infinitely more valuable than a huge amount of attention from the wrong audience.
- Still.. even now, I thought it would be more than **10**

- We did also get a feature from Steam on the front page, alongside No Mans Sky to showcase games specifically supporting the index controllers in unique ways.

## Conclusion
- I'm going to skip a lot of detail between then and the launch as it was mostly developing the game at that point to try and get something out the door.
- We ultimately did, it had a loop, it was functional, there were several dozen microgames, and it was all wrapped up in a loop about entering these VR spaces though VHS tapes.
- It also had competitive leaderboards for fastest completions of each tape. 
- I still think the vibe to this day is pretty cool, I'm proud that we nailed that. 
- But most of the microgames were not actually that fun. The showcased ones were, and they felt good, but the rest were reskins, or lacked the feedback to make them satisfying.
- Also a whole lot of bugs, there was practically no time to QA this thing.
- We did follow up with a couple patches post-launch to resolve most of the worst bugs, and add more content, but it was too late. The momentum was killed within the first couple days. 

- Realistically, we should have just pushed out the game by a few more weeks, fix the bugs, get the existing games to a polished state.
- But we were obsessed with being first to market, and it ultimately hurt the game.

- So in all, great lessons learned:
  - A small, targeted audience is better for actual growth than a massive, general one.
  - In the words of Miyamoto: *A delayed game is eventually good, but a rushed game is forever bad.*
